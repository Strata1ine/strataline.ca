Hiring trades separately can work for a clearly isolated task. It becomes harder when demolition, drywall, electrical work, flooring, painting and finish carpentry all affect the same surfaces or must happen in a precise order.

## Look for shared surfaces

If several trades open and close the same walls, work around the same flooring or depend on the same measurements, coordination becomes part of the renovation—not an administrative extra.

## Consider who owns the sequence

A complete scope gives one party responsibility for trade order, protection changes and final connections. With separate contracts, the homeowner usually becomes the coordinator and must resolve gaps between scopes.

## Separate work can suit defined tasks

One-room painting, a standalone fixture replacement or another contained service may not need a broader renovation structure. The boundaries should be clear and should not depend on unfinished work by another trade.

## Compare exclusions and handoffs

Ask who repairs opened surfaces, protects completed work, schedules inspections where required and corrects deficiencies at the boundary between trades. A lower isolated price may leave these items outside every proposal.
