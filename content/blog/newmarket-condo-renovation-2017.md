    eyebrow: Existing condition
    heading: The condo had good space—but nearly every visible finish was dated.
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        Lynne Moore and Bill Fortnum were downsizing from a larger house into an older Newmarket condo. They wanted less property maintenance, more freedom to travel and a home that was ready to enjoy. The condo offered the space they needed, but its carpet, textured ceilings, beige walls, older trim and original-looking details still gave it the unmistakable character of a 1980s interior.
      - >-
        No single finish was catastrophic. The problem was cumulative. Carpet met dated resilient flooring, repeatedly painted popcorn texture darkened the ceiling plane, and the doors, baseboards, heaters and wall colours all reinforced the same age. Changing only one surface would have left every adjoining finish looking older by comparison.

    eyebrow: One coordinated scope
    heading: Why this project had to be planned as one renovation
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        The clients' goal was not an isolated repair. They wanted the condo to feel completely renewed before they moved in, so retirement meant enjoying the home rather than immediately renovating it. That made sequence as important as the individual trades. Ceiling removal had to precede final paint. Flooring decisions affected baseboards, door clearances and transitions. Deliveries and waste removal had to fit the building's access rules.
      - >-
        Treating the work as one connected scope allowed disruptive stages to happen while the unit was empty. It also kept finish decisions coordinated: smooth ceilings, flooring, wall colour, doors, trim and heater profiles could be judged as parts of one interior rather than as unrelated upgrades. This is the same planning principle used in Strataline's [coordinated interior renovations](/interior-renovations).

    heading: The painted popcorn ceiling had to go before the rooms could feel new.
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        The ceiling texture had been painted repeatedly. Moisture could not simply penetrate it as readily as ordinary unpainted texture, so the project called for the dust-controlled mechanical approach Strataline was developing at the time.       - >-
        Removal was only the first stage. The exposed ceiling surface was checked, corrected and finished smooth before primer and ceiling paint. Eliminating the heavy texture changed how light moved through the rooms and gave the finished walls, flooring and trim a clean plane above them. Current homeowners can review the full sequence in Strataline's [popcorn ceiling removal service](/services/popcorn-removal).

    heading: The floor had to look like hardwood and work like a condo floor.
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        Once the carpet was removed, the flooring choice had to answer more than an appearance question. The condo had a concrete substrate, the building had sound-transmission requirements, and the completed floor needed stable installation, daily durability and visual continuity across the main living areas.
      - >-
        Project records identify the product as Fuzion Flooring engineered flooring. We are keeping the construction description restrained because the exact historical product sheet is not part of the current record. What can be stated confidently is that the engineered flooring was selected for installation over the condo's concrete substrate and to meet the building's acoustic requirements. No invented STC or IIC rating is needed to explain that decision.
      - >-
        Layout also mattered. Doorways, short walls and connected rooms created many starting points and sightlines. Boards had to carry cleanly through that geometry without allowing one awkward transition to dictate the whole installation. Our experience with this flooring system has been very good, both on client projects and in long-term residential use, but the project remains about the coordinated renovation rather than a product advertisement.

  - type: text
    eyebrow: Building coordination
    heading: Renovating a condo is a logistics project before it is a construction project.
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        Condo work begins with building rules, management coordination and access planning. Elevator bookings determine when large deliveries, tools and debris can move. Corridors, elevator surfaces, the unit entrance and adjoining finished areas need protection before traffic begins. Condo corporations commonly require contractors to provide insurance documentation and, where applicable, WSIB information before work begins. Requirements vary, so they must be confirmed for the specific building rather than assumed.
      - >-
        When the service elevator is reserved from 9:00 to 11:00, that is the delivery window. Missing material cannot simply be brought upstairs whenever convenient after another resident or contractor takes over the booking. Materials therefore need to be checked in advance, tools organized, protection ready and delivery sequencing planned before the reserved period starts.
      - >-
        Common-area protection is part of the renovation scope, not an optional cleanup step. Elevator walls and floors, corridors, the unit entrance and finished areas inside the condo must be protected for the kind of traffic expected. Debris also needs to remain controlled on its way out. Damage to common areas is not an acceptable part of the renovation process.
      - >-
        Technical requirements affect the work inside the unit as well. A concrete substrate changes flooring preparation and installation decisions, while the corporation's acoustic criteria influence which floor assemblies are acceptable. Coordinating those requirements early keeps product selection, delivery and installation from becoming separate last-minute problems.
