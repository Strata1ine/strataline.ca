  - eyebrow: Colour correction
    heading: Why stain matching was difficult
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        A stain sample card cannot predict a finished staircase by itself. Wood species and the natural tone of the board affect how colour is absorbed and reflected. The stain colour matters, but so do the preparation of the surface, the amount and consistency of application, the room lighting, the neighbouring floor and the protective topcoat placed over the colour.
      - >-
        Red oak has a strong natural warmth. Grey and neutral stain colours can shift red, yellow, blue or green depending on how that warmth combines with the colour layer and the light in the room. The topcoat adds another optical change. It can deepen the wood, increase contrast and change how warm or cool the same stain appears. That is why fixing the staircase required a controlled system rather than selecting another colour name and applying it over the previous work.
  - eyebrow: Dust-controlled process
    heading: Correcting the staircase without filling the house with dust
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        This was corrective work inside an already finished home, so containment and protection were part of the technical plan. We isolated the work area with dust barriers, protected the surrounding floors and organized tools and materials so the circulation areas could be kept controlled. Festool sanders were connected to dust extraction at the source during the dust-producing stages.
      - >-
        No sanding process creates literal zero airborne dust, but source extraction, barriers and disciplined cleanup substantially reduce how far it travels. The photographs document both the containment and the extraction equipment used during the project. That dust-controlled approach matters especially when refinishing a central staircase beside completed rooms and finished flooring.

  - eyebrow: Surface preparation
    heading: Sanding everything back to bare wood
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        A dependable colour match required a consistent starting surface. Where necessary, the railing was removed so the relevant stair and railing components could be reached properly. We sanded the failed stain and finish back to bare wood instead of layering another colour over material that already contained scratches, uneven absorption and several earlier stain attempts.
      - >-
        This step was critical because traces of old finish can block new stain, while over-sanded or inconsistently sanded areas can absorb colour differently. The goal was to bring the red oak back to a controlled surface, preserve the profiles and prepare adjoining components to respond to the new colour system as consistently as possible.

  - eyebrow: On-site development
    heading: Why we match stain in the actual home
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        There was no spare flooring sample available to send away for matching, and the target was the floor already installed around the staircase. We therefore developed and evaluated the colour on site, beside that floor and under the actual room lighting. This is not a claim that every outside stain shop performs poor matching; it is a response to the specific conditions of this project.
      - >-
        Shop lighting can differ substantially from daylight and interior fixtures in a home. The surrounding paint, floor sheen and direction of light also influence perception. We checked the developing colour where it would be seen, then accounted for the visual change expected after the clear protective topcoat. The objective was not an isolated stain chip. It was a staircase that coordinated with the installed floor in the finished room.
  - eyebrow: Project-specific colour system
    heading: Why the white base mattered
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        The grey tone could not read correctly while the red oak's natural red and yellow warmth remained dominant. For this staircase, we developed a two-part colour process. First, a light white base was established to reduce the strong warm influence of the oak. The grey colour layer was then applied over that controlled base.
      - >-
        The sequence was project-specific: develop a light base, neutralize enough of the oak's warmth, apply the grey layer, evaluate it beside the actual floor and account for how the clear protective topcoat would change the appearance. The white base was not a universal formula for red oak. Its role, strength and relationship to the grey layer were developed for this wood, this floor and this home's lighting.

    heading: Rebuilding the finish
    paragraphs:
      - >-
        Getting the colour close was not the end of the job. The original finish also contained sanding scratches, uneven colour, trapped debris and installation marks. Once the stain system had been developed and applied consistently, the protective finish had to be rebuilt cleanly so it would support the colour instead of exposing another set of defects.
      - >-
        Each coat was treated as part of the finished appearance. Surface cleanliness, application consistency and the optical effect of the clear finish all mattered. The renewed topcoat protected the corrected wood while producing the clean, consistent surface that should have been present from the beginning.

  - Existing finish defects must be removed before a proper new finish is built.
