A stair quote should let you understand what the finished staircase will include. A short price beside a tread count is not enough when the project also involves carpet removal, repairs, landings, paint work, railing connections and finish coordination.

Based on the stair projects we review across the GTA, quote differences usually come from scope differences—not just labour rates or material prices. The practical way to compare proposals is to identify what each contractor has included, excluded or left undefined.

## Start with the staircase geometry

The quote should identify the staircase being renovated, including the tread count and any details that change measuring or fitting.

Important geometry includes:

- straight, curved or open-sided flights;
- winders or irregular tread shapes;
- nosings and exposed tread returns;
- intermediate and upper landings;
- transitions into surrounding flooring;
- upper guards and open edges.

A curved tread or open side is not equivalent to a standard rectangular tread. If the geometry is not described, it is difficult to know whether custom work is included.

## Identify what can remain

The quote should distinguish components that are being preserved from those being replaced.

For existing stairs, that can include the underlying structure, hardwood treads, risers, stringers, posts and handrails. A proposal for [stair refinishing](/services/stairs/refinishing) should describe the condition assumed to be suitable for sanding and repair. A proposal for [hardwood tread caps](/services/stairs/tread-caps) should describe preparation of the existing stair and the new tread and nosing surface.

If carpet is still installed, the contractor may need to state what is assumed and what will be confirmed after removal.

## List repairs rather than implying them

Loose components, squeaks, cracks, damaged nosings and previous repairs do not all have the same solution. The quote should identify known corrections and explain how newly exposed conditions will be handled.

Some squeaks can be reduced during a renovation, but the source of movement and available access matter. A promise to eliminate every noise without seeing the structure is not the same as a defined repair allowance.

## Separate the visible stair components

The walking surface is only one part of the finished staircase. A complete scope should address:

- treads and nosings;
- risers;
- inside and outside stringers;
- landings and floor transitions;
- trim or skirt details where applicable.

If risers and stringers are being painted white, the quote should identify preparation, filling, selective caulking, primer and finish coats. “Paint stairs” does not explain which surfaces or level of preparation are included.

## Define the railing scope

Railing work should list the components being installed or retained:

- new posts and their locations;
- handrails and wall-mounted rails;
- baluster material and pattern;
- upper landing guards;
- connections to treads, nosings and floors;
- finishing of wood railing components.

Strataline railing renovations use new posts. Existing wood spindles are not refinished, and an existing handrail remains only when suitable for the new system. Those kinds of project-specific policies should be clear before pricing is compared.

The [stair railing installation page](/services/stairs/railing-installation) shows why the flight and upper guards should be planned as one visual and functional system.

## Explain stain and finish expectations

“Match the floor” needs more detail. Wood species, age, grain, old coatings, lighting and floor wear affect how close a relationship can be achieved.

A useful quote should identify whether stain samples are included, where they will be reviewed and which components receive stain, clear finish, primer or paint. It should also account for drying and restricted-access periods between coats.

## Include protection, access and cleanup

Stairs are circulation routes, often in occupied homes. The quote should explain:

- protection for adjacent floors and finishes;
- dust-control measures for sanding;
- how access changes during work and coating;
- furniture or household constraints near the stair;
- daily or final cleanup expectations;
- disposal of carpet, removed railings or other material when applicable.

Dust-producing work should be described as dust-controlled rather than literally dust-free.

## Show the sequence

The order of tread, paint and railing work affects the finished connections. A coordinated sequence helps prevent completed surfaces from being reopened or damaged by a later trade.

The quote does not need a day-by-day calendar before the project is scheduled, but it should establish the major sequence and explain which conditions can change timing.

## State exclusions and change conditions

Exclusions protect both parties from different assumptions. Common examples include hidden structural work, electrical changes, floor replacement beyond the landing, wall repairs outside the stair area or conditions concealed by carpet.

The quote should also explain how a newly discovered condition is documented and approved before additional work proceeds.

## A concise comparison checklist

When comparing stair quotes, place the proposals side by side and mark whether each one defines:

1. stair geometry and tread count;
2. refinishing, tread caps or replacement method;
3. carpet removal and disposal;
4. repairs and squeak work;
5. risers, stringers and landings;
6. posts, handrails, balusters and upper guards;
7. stain samples, paint preparation and finish coats;
8. occupied-home protection and dust control;
9. access restrictions, cleanup and sequence;
10. inclusions, exclusions and change conditions.

The better comparison is not simply the lowest total or price per tread. It is the proposal that most clearly describes the finished staircase and the work required to reach it.
